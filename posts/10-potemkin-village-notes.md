# Notes from a Potemkin Village: When Primary Market Actors Enter the Secondary Market

> 2026-06-27

## Preface

This is not a moral judgment. The Physics PhD, the Deputy, the Boss — they are not bad people. They simply live in a different system. The primary market has its own survival logic, and in that world, titles and performance are not flaws but necessities. Their problem is not a character flaw — it is that they **brought primary market survival skills into the secondary market**, where the rules are precisely inverted.

This is not contempt; it is understanding. Once you understand why they behaved the way they did, you understand why you could not stay. Different paths for different people.

## The Physics PhD's Survival Strategy

The Physics PhD — actually not a physics PhD at all, but an optoelectronic materials PhD. He transplanted his materials science training into quant — swapping "experimental data" for "stock data" and "group meeting presentations" for "reporting to the Boss."

His survival strategy was a closed loop:

**Draw charts.** Curves were the sole output. No statistical tests needed, no out-of-sample performance, no reproducibility. Just a chart the Boss could understand. This is not hyperbole — the codebase was physical evidence. Twenty-plus notebook files, three scripts, zero tests, zero documentation, zero version control. The filenames themselves were the methodology: one was called "copy so-and-so's quant framework," another "save chart one," "save chart two," and one simply named "look at recent" — meaning only look at recent performance. The same factor rerun the next day with a date appended to the filename indicated manual parameter tuning. Another file was named "code to modify returns" — directly altering the main program logic to make curves look good. The entire codebase contained not a single statistical test, no p-values, no confidence intervals, no out-of-sample validation. The factors used were turnover rate and non-operating income — day-one-of-quant-101 level. This isn't "he might have overfit" — it's "his methodology *is* overfitting."

**Tell stories.** Wrap the chart in words like "factor crowding," "anomaly points," "catch-up demand." No need to understand what these terms mean — they just need to sound professional at dinner.

**Enjoy the "technical authority" treatment while managing an exit.** He had already locked in his Big Tech offer; this project was more than a transition — it was a springboard. The Boss flattered him, the Deputy depended on him, and he went with the flow. But he wasn't simply enjoying being taken seriously — he was cultivating a **live track record**. He fantasized about grinding at Big Tech for a few years, saving money, combining it with this live trading experience, and rebranding himself as a "quant researcher with real-market experience" for a hedge fund transition. **Maintain dominance.** Anyone hired had to be his tool — using his framework, running his factors, drawing his charts. You couldn't propose an automated pipeline, not just because it would replace his manual tuning, but because if the strategy ran on someone else's system, the track record would no longer be "his." He needed the record to be "his" — his framework, his factors, his curves. Even if the factors were just turnover rate and non-operating income, even if the record had no statistical validation behind it. The record didn't need to be real; it just needed to look real, to be writable on a résumé, to be presentable in an interview. You were not brought in to collaborate — you were brought in to be used. Your existence threatened his "authorship" of the record.

He didn't need collapse because he had never built anything and never intended to. He could jump to the next bubble before this one burst — Big Tech was his next bubble. If the strategy failed, it wasn't his fault — it was a "black swan," a "manipulated stock," "market manipulation." He would never admit "my strategy was wrong from day one," because admitting it meant this transition period's "research" was invalid, and he had already written this experience into his résumé.

## The 5,000-Yuan Computer Budget

The Deputy allocated no more than 5,000 yuan for the Physics PhD's computer — not enough for a machine capable of factor research. It wasn't that he was stingy; he was willing to buy dinner for clients but unwilling to invest in hardware.

He probably couldn't grasp that the essence of quant is batch data falsification. It's not about a genius having a flash of insight to find a factor; it's about using compute power to feed hundreds or thousands of hypotheses into the data, letting the data kill the false ones, and only then looking twice at what survives. The core competitiveness of quant is not "smart people" — it's "how many times can you falsify."

But that's only the first layer. Falsification efficiency isn't just about "how many times" — there are three multipliers: scale of falsification × quality of falsification × width of the hypothesis space. Compute power solves scale — how many times you can run; methodology solves quality — out-of-sample testing, orthogonalization, multiple testing correction determine whether each falsification is valid; 1,000 wrong backtests are worth less than 100 correct ones. The research system solves width — what to falsify; if the hypothesis space is impoverished, no amount of falsification is anything more than fishing in a small pond.

This still requires "intelligence," but not the intelligence of "thinking up good factors" — rather, the intelligence of "knowing how to eliminate bad ones." This intelligence manifests not in flashes of insight but in framework design — and once the framework is designed, it can be executed at scale by compute power. So compute power is not a replacement for smart people; it's a lever for smart people — provided the person knows what to use compute power to falsify. The Deputy couldn't understand any of this. He saw only "people"; he couldn't see "systems."

He would probably attribute quant results entirely to people. In his mental model, whether a strategy is good depends on "whether the person doing it is capable" — computer speed is irrelevant. This is consistent with primary market logic: whether a project succeeds depends on "how strong your connections are," not what tools you use. So he'd spend 5,000 yuan on a dinner for someone who might bring a project, but wouldn't spend 5,000 yuan to add RAM to a computer. In his ledger, people are worth more than machines — because people can drink, toast, and say "this project looks good" at the dinner table. A computer just quietly runs code and tells you: this factor has stopped working. That wasn't the answer he wanted.

He didn't need a faster computer; he needed a faster performance.

The Deputy once called the Physics PhD's backtest script a "system." I paused when I heard it. Something without proper function encapsulation, without tests, without documentation, without version control — called a "system." But I didn't correct him. For one thing, what the Physics PhD had written didn't even qualify as a good script, so explaining the difference between "script" and "system" would be casting pearls before swine. For another, the misnomer itself was diagnostic — in the Deputy's cognition, any code that produces a curve is a "system." He didn't know what a system was, but he needed a word for the "thing that produces curves," and "system" sounded more prestigious than "script," more valuable, more like something that could be reported as a business unit.

Calling a backtest script a "system" and treating a 5,000-yuan computer as quant infrastructure are two symptoms of the same blind spot: he can't distinguish tools from systems, just as he can't distinguish people from methodology.

## The Structural Cause of Death for Primary-to-Secondary Transitions

Primary and secondary markets are completely different games. Primary: relationships → information asymmetry → exclusive channels → profit. Secondary: data → statistical testing → out-of-sample performance → profit.

Firms that attempt primary-to-secondary transitions all die in the same place: they assume resources and relationships transfer. They don't — the rules have changed. The old moat — connections, networks, exclusive channels — is worthless in the new arena. The market doesn't care who your shareholders are or how wide your network is; it only cares whether your model is correct. Using primary market thinking to do secondary market work is like eating hotpot with a knife and fork — it's not a matter of form; the tool is wrong.

## The Business Model of Pseudo-Quant Funds

The core of these funds isn't doing quant; it's using the word "quant" to collect management fees. Management fees are the only guaranteed income — whether the strategy makes money is a probability event, but management fees are a certainty. You don't need to get quant right; you just need clients to believe you're doing quant. As long as clients don't redeem, the management fee accrues daily.

And the "smart wealthy" are the easiest to fool — because they believe they're smart. So when the story is sexy enough, the presenter confident enough, and the curve attractive enough, they won't suspect they're being deceived. They'll suspect that "the market is bad," that "the strategy needs time," that "this drawdown is normal." They'll use their own intelligence to help the fraudster explain why they lost money.

## The Primary Market's Trust Structure

Why does the primary market naturally reject validation? Because its entire trust structure is built on people, not methods.

The primary market is a highly uncertain environment. You invest in an unprofitable company, an unvalidated business model, a story that might not survive to the next round. In this environment, math is useless — there's no historical data, no statistical significance. The only thing you can grasp is the person: Is this person reliable? Is their track record good? Do they speak with charisma?

So the primary market developed a trust system anchored on people:

**Titles replace validation.** The Physics PhD's doctoral title didn't need verification — "physics PhD" is itself a signal. Whether he studied optoelectronic materials or theoretical physics, the audience doesn't care and won't check.

**Aura replaces evidence.** Someone who speaks animatedly at a dinner is more credible than someone who quietly writes code — because in the primary market, "confidence" is capability. You can't evaluate his model, but you can evaluate his presence.

**Relationships replace methodology.** Who said something matters more than whether what they said is accurate. "A researcher from a macro hedge fund said this" — the logic is: because he's in that circle, what he says is correct. This is a trust chain, not an evidence chain.

The primary market's lack of reverence for math isn't arrogance — it's fear. If mathematical validation were introduced, the entire trust chain would collapse — you'd discover that every project you invested in had unclear endogeneity problems, that every founder you believed in had a résumé that couldn't withstand scrutiny. Math isn't a tool; math is a mirror. And the primary market doesn't need a mirror — it needs a makeup artist.

This is why the Physics PhD survived in the primary market. He wasn't providing quant capability — he was providing makeup services. His PhD title was the Boss's foundation, his overfitted curves were the Boss's concealer, his animated gestures were the Boss's lipstick.

And my presence made them uncomfortable — not because I did anything wrong, but because I stood next to the makeup artist holding a mirror. I asked "does this factor have statistical significance," I asked "does this strategy have out-of-sample testing" — every question I asked was essentially asking "is this makeup fake?" Standing next to a makeup artist and exposing the makeup is, naturally, unwelcome.

## This Pattern Extends Beyond the Potemkin Village

Students don't attend to evaluate "is this model correct" but to feel "this person is impressive" — titles, aura, presence, no different from the Physics PhD's animated gestures. What they need isn't knowledge; it's reassurance: if the big shot says so, my thesis direction must be fine, right? If the big shot makes this judgment, I can cite it in my interview, right?

But here's the thing — the big shot himself has probably read few annual reports. The chief economist's core skill isn't forecasting; it's narrative. He needs to compress complex economic phenomena into a story tellable at a dinner. Precision doesn't matter; transmissibility does. This is exactly the same as the Physics PhD reducing quant to "save chart" — it's not that they don't want to get it right; it's that their audience doesn't require them to get it right.

When the audience doesn't need validation, the performer doesn't need to be correct.

But to be honest, statisticians aren't necessarily more virtuous than performers. Everyone fundamentally needs something from the audience — the chief economist needs adoration, the Physics PhD needs resources and dominance, the Boss needs clients' money. The difference is only in tools: some perform, some use statistics. Statistics can produce genuine insights, but it can also be used to manufacture more sophisticated illusions — p-hacking, data mining, overfitting are all forms of statistical performance.

The only real difference is this — statistics are honest, at least falsifiable. You can dislike a p-value, but you can't make it disappear. You can change the window, change the sample, change the model specification, but every operation leaves traces — reviewers can find them, readers can reproduce them, future researchers can overturn them. The fundamental contract of statistics isn't "I prove I'm right" but "I tell you how I did it, you can reproduce it under the same conditions, and if the results differ, I'm wrong."

Performance has no such contract. The Physics PhD's overfitted curves don't need to tell you how parameters were tuned; the chief economist's lecture doesn't need to tell you how many times predictions were wrong; the Boss's dinner doesn't need to tell you whether that "catch-up demand" ever actually caught up. Their assertions are unfalsifiable — not because they're too smart, but because they never provided conditions that could be tested.

## The Mirror Effect

I might not have said anything, but my presence alone made them uncomfortable. The Deputy and the Physics PhD loved to pontificate about their market operations — "I called this one right," "I have experience with that factor." Meanwhile, I sat quietly writing code, building pipelines, running backtests.

My existence was itself a negation of their narrative. I didn't need to say a word; my way of working said "whatever you're claiming, I can verify with code." When someone is bragging and a person who can check the claims is standing right there, the bragging stops.

So they needed me to disappear — not physically, but in terms of voice. No computer for you, no pipeline for you, no automation for you. Not because you did anything wrong, but because you did something right. Your presence made it impossible for them to lie.

## Departure

I didn't leave voluntarily. The Deputy told me: quant is done; we're disbanding.

I wasn't fired — the entire quant operation was cut. The Physics PhD went to Huawei, the Deputy returned to subjective trading, the Boss returned to the primary market. They dispersed not because someone left, but because this organization had never actually done quant. Disbanding was simply a return to its original state.

And I — the moment I received the dissolution notice, I felt relief.

I didn't need this job to prove myself; I already had my own work. This job wasn't a livelihood; it was the runtime environment for a diagnostic program. The diagnostic finished, the conclusion was output, the runtime environment closed — this wasn't failure; it was a normal exit.

I wasn't driven out, and I didn't flee. I had already completed what I needed to do before they disbanded. When they dissolved, I was already packing my things.

I left a broken organization and then used it as a counterexample to build an entire correct system. This wasn't "leaving" — it was distilling error into methodology.

## In the End

Ultimately, the wall between primary market players and quant isn't a methodological wall — it's a worldview wall. In their world, fluency can substitute for rigor, connections can substitute for ability, and capital can substitute for cognition.

I was there. I saw it. I left.

Any information that cannot be independently verified is, in essence, noise. This is the most important thing I learned at the Potemkin village.
