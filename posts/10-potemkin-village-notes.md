# Notes from a Potemkin Village: When Primary Market Actors Enter the Secondary Market

> 2026-06-27

## Preamble

What follows is not a moral judgment. The PhD, the Deputy, the Boss — they are not bad people. They simply live in a different system. The primary market has its own survival logic; in that world, titles and performance are not flaws but necessities. Their problem is not character — it is that **they brought primary-market survival skills into the secondary market**, and the rules of these two markets are inverses of each other.

This is not contempt; it is understanding. To understand why they did what they did is to understand why I could not stay. Each to their own way of living.

---

## What Kind of Company

The roots are in the primary market. The firm attached itself to the Boss's relationship resources to run projects; its core moat is connections and networks. It later tried to pivot, using primary-market thinking and personnel to do secondary-market quantitative trading. The personnel structure: the Boss (relationship resources), the Deputy (a relationship hire from the shareholders, accounting background, de facto business-manager role, zero technical ability), the PhD (an intern who had already locked in a Big Tech offer, formally elevated by the Boss as the "technical authority").

Three tiers of people, three tiers of logic, one thing in common — none of them actually understood quant.

The capital structure of the experimental book was delicate: all three seem to have put in money. The Boss's share was likely the largest, the Deputy's next, the PhD's the smallest. This structure determined each one's motive.

The Deputy put in money as classic primary-market logic — men on the same boat must dare to bet, dare to size up, to prove sincerity and resolve. His stake was not merely to build a track record; it was to **consolidate control** over the quant business. He did not need to seize this seat — the Boss already habitually called him into the office alone; he played a de facto business-manager role in the firm. The quant business naturally fell under his "jurisdiction." At every Monday meeting, he was the first to report to the Boss on "strategy progress," "factor performance," "next steps." He did not understand quant, but he did not need to — accounting background, zero technical ability, but he needed to sit in the seat of "head of quant." Putting in money made him feel not merely that he was "participating," but that he was "governing."

The PhD's motive was more complex than it appeared. He did need to prove to the Boss that he "dared to bet" — but "daring to bet" was not the goal; the goal was **to build a live track record**. He had been heard loudly holding forth in the office about how certain hedge-fund interviews ask "how big a live book have you run," about what kind of résumé a million-RMB-salary role requires. These were not casual remarks — he was planning his exit. Big Tech was not the destination; it was the starting point. He fantasized about accumulating a live performance record at this Potemkin village, then leveraging it to jump to a hedge fund, transitioning from "optoelectronics-materials PhD" to "quant expert."

So his stake was not merely a credential of trust — it was **a career-transition investment**. He needed this book to exist, to "look like it was making money," to produce a record that could be written into a future résumé. Why would a man about to leave for Big Tech care about a Potemkin experimental book? Because Big Tech was not his endpoint — it was a savings depot, and the Potemkin live record was his transition springboard. He likely told himself: grind a few years at Big Tech, save money, add this live experience, and then re-invent himself as "a quant researcher with live-trading experience."

This makes his behavior at the firm more legible. He opposed the automated pipeline not only to save face as the "technical authority," but because automation would strip him of control over the book — if strategies ran on a system someone else had built, his "live record" would no longer be "his." He needed the record to be *his* — his framework, his factors, his curves. Even if those factors were just turnover and non-operating income. Even if the record had no statistical validation behind it. The record did not need to be true; it only needed to look true, good enough to put on a résumé.

The Boss also put in money, probably the most. But his attitude toward P&L was the most relaxed — because he had exits on both sides. He needed a quant-curve-supported marketing wrapper: if the strategy actually made money, he could charge management fees and pivot from primary to secondary; if it did not, he could always retreat to primary speculation, and the experimental-book money would be booked as marketing expense. Either way, the story "we are doing quant" was worth the price of admission.

A small incident during a drawdown period confirmed this. The PhD once privately asked the Deputy what the Boss and the shareholders really thought about quant. The Deputy said: "They probably just treat it as an experiment." That sentence carries a lot — "experiment" means it can be dropped at any time, "probably" means even the Boss himself had not taken it seriously. The PhD's reaction is unknown, but he did not leave, did not change methodology; he kept drawing curves and telling stories. Because what he needed was not for the strategy to make money — it was for this live record to last one more day. The Boss's "experiment" mind-set and the PhD's "record management" aligned perfectly in that moment — both sides knew the thing might fail, but both needed it to keep existing.

The Boss's capital bought the right to use the word "quant" and a two-way exit, not validation of the strategy.

## Capital = Illusion of Control

The primary-market logic is: he who pays has the say. This logic was transplanted into the secondary market. The Deputy put in capital and felt entitled to define "how quant should be done" — "quant can just use your own computer, no?" His understanding of quant stopped at "looking at K-lines on a computer"; but paying made him feel he had adjudication rights over something he did not understand.

And this illusion was not something he seized — it was given to him by the Boss. The Boss already habitually called him into the office alone; he played a de facto business-manager role. Accounting background, zero technical ability, but the Boss needed someone to "manage" the things he himself could not read. The quant business naturally fell under this "business manager." At every Monday meeting, he was first to report to the Boss on "strategy progress," "factor performance," "next steps." None of those words were his — they were the PhD's. But he stood before the Boss representing the quant team. He did not understand what those curves meant, but he said "this direction is good," "we need to keep optimizing" — these sentences require no understanding of quant to say, but saying them was equivalent to declaring "this is my territory." The PhD did the research; the Deputy did the reporting. One supplied technology, the other supplied mouth. But in the Boss's eyes, the one who reports is the "person in charge" — because reporting implies control, and control implies jurisdiction.

Capital gave him control, but control cannot substitute for cognition. He was controlling something he did not understand, and the result was that control became a shackle — binding him to the wrong methodology. Worse, his capital had a clear purpose: to build a track record that would attract follow-on investment from the Boss and entrench his identity as "head of quant." This meant he could not accept drawdowns, because a drawdown would destroy the narrative by which he asked the Boss for money and for the seat. So he instinctively preferred methods that "looked like they were making money" — and overfit curves are precisely the ones most skilled at "looking like they are making money." The more money he put in, the less he could tolerate the truth.

The PhD also put in money — the smallest share, but the most complex motive. On the surface, his stake was a "dared to bet" trust signal. In reality, he was buying **a live record** — he needed to write "has live-trading experience" on his résumé, needed to say "I have run a live book" in future hedge-fund interviews. His stake was not the source of control; it was **an admission ticket for career transition**. The signal he sent to the Boss was: "I dare to bet my own money, so I believe in my strategy." But the signal he truly wanted to send to future employers was: "I have done live trading, I have a performance record." This signal had a fatal logical hole — his record had no statistical tests, no out-of-sample validation, no reproducibility. But the record did not need to be true; it only needed to look true. A small sum of money bought him, simultaneously, the Boss's trust and future interview material.

The Boss's flattery was more dangerous than capital — capital at least was real interest, while flattery traded illusory authority for compliance. I could never tell whether the Boss's flattery was sincere or cynical. Sincere: he genuinely believed the PhD understood quant, treated him as a technical pillar. Cynical: he knew perfectly well how shallow the PhD was, but needed a "technical authority" placeholder so shareholders could see "we have a PhD." Perhaps both — the Boss himself did not really understand quant; he lacked the capacity to judge whether the PhD was an authority, so he could only choose to believe. In the primary market, you do not need to verify whether someone actually knows; you only need to decide whether to believe him. And the primary market has always preferred the confident, the silver-tongued, the "leadership-persona" types — because, to put it bluntly, everyone is a fraud, and only the successful ones get re-packaged as business gurus. In a den of frauds, verification is self-destruction; belief is the survival strategy. The Boss chose to believe, or chose to pretend to believe — because, true or false, "our PhD is doing quant" required a "PhD" standing there. The form was achieved; the function was realized.

True or false, the effect was the same — an intern about to leave was endowed with unwarranted technical adjudication power, and used a small stake to gild himself with "dares to bet." He opposed the automated pipeline not because his money would lose, but because the identity of "technical authority" made him feel qualified to adjudicate what was correct quant and what was wrong quant.

## Symbiosis

The Boss needed the PhD's title to package the "we have quant" story, so he formally elevated him to technical authority — sincere or cynical, it did not matter. The Deputy needed the PhD to prove that he "had quant" — he did not understand quant, but he needed a "knower" to front the operation. The PhD needed this stage — he had already locked in his Big Tech offer, but before leaving, the "technical authority" identity let him enjoy a stretch of being taken seriously. His hand-tuning was no match for an automated pipeline, but the Boss's trust and the Deputy's dependence gave him shelter.

The three endorsed one another: the Boss said "our PhD is doing quant"; the Deputy said "quant needs experienced judgment"; the PhD said "this direction is good." Anyone trying to build an automated system simultaneously dismantled all three parties' platforms — the Boss would discover that "technical authority" output was just overfit curves; the Deputy would discover that quant did not need "experienced judgment"; the PhD would discover his hand-tuning was replaceable. So they defended jointly: one opposed your pipeline, one did not support your computer, one pretended not to see the problem. All the same thing in essence — keep your system from being built, and their symbiosis remained safe.

The deeper cause: they did not know how, and did not want to learn. These people had no patience for deep research; they feared math and engineering. If quant became fully mathematical and automated, it would inevitably escape their control — they could not read the models, could not modify the code, could not intervene in decisions. Preserving the "experienced judgment" narrative was not protecting quant; it was protecting their own voice. Math and engineering were not tools to them — they were threats.

## The PhD's Survival Strategy

The PhD — actually not a physics PhD at all, but an optoelectronics-materials PhD. Optoelectronics is materials science, an experimental discipline: run experiments, measure data, plot curves, publish papers, give PowerPoint at group meetings. It has nothing to do with theoretical physics or mathematical physics. His full skill set was overfitting, plotting curves, making slide decks. He transplanted his materials-science training into quant — swapping "experimental data" for "stock data," "group-meeting reports" for "reports to the Boss."

His survival strategy was a closed loop:

**Monologue.** In any meeting, he was guaranteed to be the one who spoke the longest. This was not extroversion; it was territory-marking. A meeting is a power field — whoever's mouth does not stop occupies the space. His monologue needed no content support, only rhythm support: every sentence delivered with certainty, every judgment phrased as a conclusion, every pause waiting for agreement. The Boss could not follow the content, but could read "confidence"; the Deputy could not follow the content, but could hear "density of jargon." Each, through his own receiving mode, confirmed the same thing — "he is speaking, therefore he is doing, therefore he is producing." Monologue is the cheapest disguise for output: no code, no tests, no out-of-sample performance required — only a glib tongue and the nerve to not be interrupted. And no one in the meeting would interrupt someone the Boss had elevated as "technical authority" — so his speaking time had no upper bound, curtailed only at the moment he himself chose to stop. He defined the length of the meeting, he allocated the weight of topics, he set the order of speakers — he needed no title; the monologue itself was the throne.

**Plotting.** Curves were the sole output. No statistical tests, no out-of-sample performance, no reproducibility required. Only one chart the Boss could understand. This is not hyperbole — the codebase is the physical evidence. Twenty-odd notebooks, three scripts, zero tests, zero documentation, zero version control. The filenames themselves were the methodology: one was called "copy so-and-so's quant framework"; others were "save plot one," "save plot two"; one filename literally read "look at recent" — meaning look only at recent performance. The same factor, re-run the next day with a date suffix on the filename — manual parameter tuning. Another file was named "modify the returns code" — directly altering the main program's logic to make the curve look good. The entire codebase contained not a single statistical test, no p-value, no confidence interval, no out-of-sample validation. The factors used were turnover and non-operating income — quant-101 level. This is not "he might be overfitting"; it is "his methodology is itself overfitting."

**Storytelling.** Packaging the chart with words like "factor crowding," "catch-up demand." No need to understand what those words meant; they only needed to sound professional at dinner.

**Enjoying the "technical authority" treatment while running an exit.** He had locked in Big Tech; this project was not merely a transition — it was a transition springboard. The Boss flattered him, the Deputy leaned on him, and he went with the flow. But he was not simply enjoying being taken seriously; he was running **a live record**. He fantasized about grinding a few years at Big Tech, saving money, adding this live experience, and re-inventing himself as "a quant researcher with live-trading experience." This fantasy made him work even harder to defend his methodology — not to do quant right, but to protect the "authorship" of this record.

**Defending dominance.** Anyone hired had to be his tool — use his framework, run his factors, draw his charts. You could not propose an automated pipeline, not only because it would replace his hand-tuning, but because if strategies ran on someone else's system, this live record would no longer be "his." He needed the record to be *his* — his framework, his factors, his curves. Even if the factors were just turnover and non-operating income, even if the record had no statistical validation behind it. The record did not need to be true; it only needed to look true, good enough to write on a résumé, good enough to bring out in an interview. You were not there to collaborate; you were there to be used — your presence threatened the "authorship" of his record.

He did not need collapse, because he had never built anything, and never intended to build here. He could jump to the next bubble before this one burst — Big Tech was his next bubble. Strategy failed? Not his fault — it was "a black swan," "a manipulated stock," "market manipulation." He would never admit "my strategy was wrong from day one," because admission would mean this transitional "research" was invalid, and he had already written this episode into his résumé.

But there is a more pathetic reading — he may not have been performing at all. He may have genuinely believed.

This is a judgment that only surfaced after repeated observation. If he had been performing, he would have been more flexible — when the curve broke, switch to a different pitch; when the Boss was absent, dial down the show; when falsified, adjust the methodology. But he was not. His manual parameter tuning, his monologues, his resistance to the automated pipeline, his grip on the Leetcode judge's seat — these were coherent, stable, invariant across scenes. A performer conserves energy; a believer does not. The excitement he showed in the office when talking about factors did not look fake — that kind of excitement needs a sincere core to keep outputting; pure performance burns out.

This means his cognitive mismatch with quant was not tactical but structural. He transplanted his materials-science training into quant — run experiments, measure data, plot curves, publish papers, present at group meetings — and that workflow *was* "research" in his cognition; he had never encountered another research paradigm. He did not know statistical tests existed, did not know why out-of-sample validation was necessary, did not know overfitting was a nameable risk — these concepts were not "known but chosen to be ignored," they **simply did not exist in his conceptual space**. He was not avoiding falsification; he did not know falsification was a thing. The curves he drew were "research results" in his world, just like the optoelectronic-materials curves he had drawn during his PhD — all data, all plots, all deliverables that could be reported.

This is the most pathetic part. A performer at least knows he is acting and needs an audience to confirm the performance; a believer needs no audience — he is his own confirmation loop. The PhD did not need the Boss's nod to believe he was doing right — he had believed it before the Boss nodded. The Boss's flattery and the Deputy's echoing were not rewards for his performance; they were **reinforcements of a pre-existing belief**. He was not confident because he was flattered; he was flatterable because he was already confident.

This makes "falsification" almost impossible. Falsification presupposes that the other party holds a belief that evidence can shake — but the PhD's belief was not propositional, it was paradigmatic. He did not believe "the turnover factor works" as a specific proposition; he believed "drawing curves = doing research" as a meta-proposition. Falsify the turnover factor and he will pick another factor and keep drawing curves; falsify one curve and he will draw the next. You cannot shake with evidence a belief that does not rest on evidence — just as you cannot convince a geocentrist with evidence, because he does not acknowledge your evidence format in the first place.

And after he goes to Big Tech, this paradigm will almost certainly not change. Big Tech will not teach him statistical tests, will not teach him a falsification framework — he will be assigned to some engineering group, keep drawing curves, making slides, reporting at group meetings, only swapping "stock data" back for "experiment data" or "product data." The "draw charts, tell stories" methodology he learned at the Potemkin village will follow him to Big Tech and be reactivated by some local engineering need there — he may sincerely believe he is "doing research" at Big Tech, just as he sincerely believed he was "doing quant" here.

This is the most pathetic thing about him — **he is not a con artist; he is worse than a con artist, he is a sincere ignoramus**. A con artist at least knows at night that he conned someone; the PhD does not. He monologues, draws charts, tells stories, resists automation every day — not because he is calculating, but because he genuinely thinks it is right. He lives in a self-consistent world where statistical tests have no place, out-of-sample has no place, "I might be wrong" has no place. He will not collapse, because his world has no entrance through which collapse could come — every signal that might collapse him is automatically filtered by his paradigm as "noise," "black swan," "manipulated stock."

I wrote his survival strategy as a "closed loop"; the word is actually not precise enough — a closed loop presupposes some link being a conscious strategy. He is not a closed loop, he is **sealed**. A closed loop still has entrances and exits; a seal does not. He is sealed inside a self-consistent cognitive pod — outside signals cannot get in, internal beliefs cannot get out. This is why my ultimate feeling toward him is not anger but a pity kept at a distance — anger presupposes he had a choice; he did not. He did not choose ignorance; he is ignorance itself.

## The Performance Relationship of Mutual Use

The Boss needed the PhD's title — primary-market storytelling demands a sexy persona, and "physics PhD doing quant" was packaging for shareholders. He did not need the PhD to actually produce a profitable strategy; he needed an impressive-looking person standing next to him so he could say "we have a PhD doing quant." So he flattered him, gave him the title of "technical authority," and let an intern wield unwarranted influence. The Boss also put in experimental-book money, probably the most — but to him this was the production cost of the "quant story." If the strategy made money, collect management fees; if not, retreat to primary speculation. Both roads worked; the money was never wasted.

The PhD needed the Boss's stage — he had locked in Big Tech, but before officially starting, this was a ready-made performance venue. There was an audience (the Boss and the Deputy), applause ("our PhD is impressive"), and authority (technical adjudication power). He did not need the Boss to understand quant; he needed someone who would not press for details, would not demand validation, and would let him keep drawing curves, telling stories, performing "I am doing research." He put in a small sum of experimental-book capital, but that was a "dared to bet" credential, not a real investment — he was about to leave; the long-term P&L of the book was irrelevant to him.

But he was not entirely indifferent to whether the strategy made money. He cared about a different kind of "making money" — the curve needed to look like it was making money, the story needed to hold together, the book needed to keep its head above water, or his authority could not hold. If the curve collapsed, the bottom fell out of his monologue-and-gesticulation. He spoke of factors in the office with performative energy, and that excitement needed a "looks all right" curve to support it. If the strategy actually made money he might not see an extra cent; but if the strategy looked bad, he lost the right to gesticulate in front of the Boss and the Deputy. So he cared — not about the money, but about the narrative that authority depended on not collapsing.

And the Boss's "use" of the PhD did not end with the performance before his departure. When the experimental book had only just started running and the PhD was about to leave for Big Tech, the Boss and another shareholder repeatedly pulled him into meeting rooms — I could not hear everything, but the keywords I caught were "Big Tech," "AI," "share." The meaning was clear: they wanted the PhD to "steal skills" at Big Tech and bring its "AI magic" back to transfuse into this Potemkin village. The offer was profit-sharing — classic pie-painting bait: carve out a slice of future, hypothetical profits, bind the person first, and leave the questions of whether it would actually make money, how much, and how to calculate it all undefined. The only thing that mattered was making the PhD feel that "what I learn at Big Tech can be monetized here later."

That move exposed the Boss's cognitive structure thoroughly. In his eyes, Big Tech was not a company that produced real research and real products — it was a mine of "AI magic." Send someone in to dig, and whatever came out could be monetized. He did not understand how Big Tech's actual work was organized, did not understand that AI was not an isolated, stealable skill, did not understand that what the PhD would do at Big Tech was likely, like the "quant research" he touted here, some local engineering rather than sorcery. He looked at Big Tech with primary-market logic — Big Tech was just an upgraded version of his relationship network; getting in meant "meeting people, learning skills, grabbing resources," and coming back meant converting all of it into his own projects. That logic may have held in the circles he knew, but applied to Big Tech it was a category error.

The PhD's response is even more worth savoring. He asked one question: "What if it doesn't make money?"

I only caught that sentence faintly at the time, but the more I thought about it afterward, the more interesting it became. The PhD was not questioning the strategy, not questioning the profit-sharing scheme — he was leaving himself an exit. He was asking about downside protection. The question revealed his own judgment: he did not actually believe the book could make money, and he believed even less in the path of "stealing AI skills from Big Tech to feed back this Potemkin village." What he needed to confirm was, if it did not make money, whether he would bear anything, how much he would bear. A person who truly believed the strategy would make money would not ask that question; a person who truly believed "AI magic" could be monetized would not ask it either. He asked it because he did not believe it himself. But he did not walk away — he kept talking, because for him this path was a pure option: if it made money, take the cut; if it did not, treat it as if it never happened, and his Big Tech salary was unaffected. Once downside protection was negotiated, the option could be held at no cost.

The Boss and the shareholder probably did not grasp the real meaning of the question. In their logic, "what if it doesn't make money" was a signal of insufficient confidence that needed encouragement; they would keep painting pies, keep promising, keep binding him with the rhetoric of "the future is huge." What they did not know was that the question itself was the PhD's implicit falsification of the entire narrative — he was using one question to tell them: "I don't believe this can make money either; I'm just confirming the boundary of the downside."

Whether that "Big Tech skill-stealing" path ever panned out, I do not know. The PhD left, the book kept running, the curves kept getting drawn, the story kept being told — but the very existence of that path showed that the Boss's understanding of "technology" and "talent" had never left the primary market: connections, profit-sharing, pie-painting, binding. He was never doing quant; he was running a connections company packaged as "quant."

The Deputy put in more money than the PhD and was the most anxious. He needed a track record to attract follow-on investment from the Boss, so he needed the PhD's curve to "look like it was making money," needed the "technical authority" to endorse his subjective judgment, needed the book's story to hold together. His stake made him the most fragile link in this performance chain — but what ultimately broke him might not be the drawdown, but the empty promise of "head of quant." He put in money, put in effort, ran the business the Boss could not read for him, reported first at every Monday meeting — only to discover the Boss "probably just treated it as an experiment." The seat was empty, the promise was hollow, the money was real.

The three satisfied one another's needs, forming a symbiosis that required no real output. The Boss got "quant story" material and a two-way exit; the PhD got the "technical authority" experience and a live record; the Deputy got the illusion of "we are building a track record" and the seat of "head of quant." Best of all — none of the three was accountable for results. The Boss did not care whether the strategy made money, because if it did he collected fees and if it did not he retreated to primary speculation; the PhD did not care whether the strategy actually made money, but he cared that the curve still looked like it was holding up, because that was the fuel for his monologue-and-gesticulation; the Deputy cared, but his caring made him the most easily manipulated — the harder he tried to build a track record, the less he could accept the truth. One needed no result, one needed the narrative not to collapse, one dared not face the result. This symbiosis naturally did not need validation.

When the US–Iran conflict began, the PhD's experimental book started bleeding steadily. The Boss's response was — he stopped attending the Monday meeting.

That move carried a great deal of information. He was not busy, not traveling, not called away — he was, after the drawdowns began, persistently absenting himself from the Monday meeting, a ritual that had been fixed on the calendar. The Monday meeting was this Potemkin village's theater of power: the Boss sat there receiving reports, the Deputy spoke first to display "jurisdiction," the PhD used monologue to occupy time. The meeting itself was the most important performance of the story "we are doing quant" — the Boss's attendance meant the story was still being taken seriously; his absence meant the story had been quietly defunded.

But he did not announce anything. Announcing would mean making a decision — keep investing or pull out? Admit failure or try once more? Each option forced him to confront the fact of the drawdown, and confronting the drawdown forced him to judge what was actually going on with the PhD's curve — which he could not do. He had never judged it; he had only chosen to believe. Now that losses had arrived and "choosing to believe" had grown expensive, he chose "not to be there." The beauty of management-by-disappearance is that it requires no decision, no explanation, no admission that "the person I anointed does not actually understand quant." He only had to skip the meeting and let the ritual slowly lose its meaning on its own.

For the Deputy, this was the cruelest signal. His first-Monday report was always performed for the Boss — with the Boss present, his "head of quant" identity had an audience; with the Boss absent, who was he reporting to? He kept reporting, but the audience had shrunk to the PhD and a few people who could not understand it. The report had lost its function — no longer "proving to the Boss that I am running quant," it had become "proving to the air that I am still running quant." But he could not stop — stopping meant admitting the Boss had given up, admitting the seat of "head of quant" was no longer recognized by anyone. He could only keep performing, even to an empty house.

For the PhD, the Boss's absence was a double loss. The primary audience for his monologue was the Boss — the Boss could not follow the content but could read "confidence," and the Boss's nod was the ultimate endorsement of his authority. With the Boss gone, the monologue lost its most important receiver. Worse, the absence itself was a verdict on his curve — if the curve were still fine, the Boss would not have left; the Boss leaving meant the Boss himself no longer believed the curve. All of his "looks like it is making money" had lost its last certifier. But he could not stop either — stopping meant admitting the strategy had failed, and he was still curating this live record for future employers. He could only keep drawing charts, keep telling stories, keep monologuing on a stage that had lost its audience.

With absence, the Boss finally cashed in his "two-way exit." He did not announce "quant has failed," did not fault the PhD's methodology, did not hold the Deputy accountable for management — he simply stopped showing up to the meeting. This preserved the surface narrative of "we are still experimenting" while in action he had already retreated to the "primary speculation" side. He did not announce it because announcing would force him to face the shareholders, face the money already sunk in, face the question "was I conned by the PhD?" — a question he did not want to answer. Disappearance is cheaper than announcement — it demands neither a decision nor an accounting. At the lowest possible cost he completed the retreat from "quant story" to "primary speculation," disguising the retreat as "busy" or "no time."

This is the final form of the symbiotic system — not a dramatic collapse, but the audience quietly leaving while the actors keep performing, until the theater empties, the lights go down, and everyone goes home. The Boss left first; the PhD is still on stage monologuing; the Deputy is still in the front row chiming in, "this direction is good" — a performance with no audience, performed for the air, performed for themselves, performed for the hallucination that "we are still doing quant."

At the last lunch before I left, the Deputy brought up the Boss's old subjective-trade doubling operation. He was not psyching himself up; he was transmitting a signal — "only subjective trading dares to bet big and make big money." On the surface, he was recalling the Boss's glorious track record; in fact, he was paving the way to rationalize the dissolution of the quant business. He was saying: quant failing was not an ability problem; subjective trading is simply more suited to making money than quant; quant was just an experiment, and returning to subjective was a "choice," not a "defeat." He needed this narrative to land first — not failure, but "choosing to return to subjective." This was not a judgment; it was a signal. He was passing on a message for the Boss, and finding himself a face-saving exit at the same time.

## The 5000-Yuan Computer Budget

The Deputy set the PhD's computer budget at no more than 5000 yuan — not even enough for a machine that could do factor research. He was not stingy; he was willing to buy dinners, unwilling to invest in hardware.

His attitude toward me was more direct — until I left, the company never assigned me a formal computer. I used my own laptop throughout, even brought my own monitor. I submitted a purchase budget; the Deputy hesitated and cut it in half. I did not raise it again. Not out of magnanimity, but because I had read the signal — a "head of quant" who would halve a researcher's computer budget was not managing a quant business, he was keeping books with an accountant's instinct. He did not think computers were worth the money. In his cognition, quant only needed a machine that could run the PhD's scripts — anything more was less cost-effective than buying dinners. This was not malice; it was ignorance. He did not know quant needed compute, just as he did not know quant needed statistical tests — what he cut was not a budget, but a need he fundamentally did not understand.

More interesting was his performance. Whenever my project made progress, he would put on a show of complaining to finance: "Why hasn't the computer procurement come through yet?" — as if it were stuck in process, as if it were not his fault. But in fact, he had cut the budget, he controlled the process. He was not pushing for the computer; he was performing "I am also anxious" — the progress was real, the complaint was fake. He needed an audience to see him "trying," but did not need the computer to actually arrive. Because arrival meant spending, and spending meant choosing between "investing in hardware" and "buying dinners," and he would always choose the latter.

He probably could not grasp that the essence of quant is batch data falsification. Not a genius having a flash of insight to find a factor, but using compute to pour hundreds or thousands of hypotheses in, letting the data kill the false ones, and only then looking twice at the survivors. The core competitiveness of quant is not "smart people"; it is "how many falsifications can you run."

But that is only the first layer. The efficiency of falsification is not just "how many"; there are three multipliers: **scale of falsification × quality of falsification × width of hypothesis space**. Compute solves scale — how many runs; methodology solves quality — out-of-sample testing, orthogonalization, multiple-testing correction, which determine whether each falsification is valid (a thousand wrong backtests is worse than a hundred right ones); the research system solves width — what to falsify; if the hypothesis space is impoverished, no amount of falsification is more than fishing in a small pond.

This still requires "smarts," but not "smarts at thinking up good factors" — rather, "smarts at knowing how to eliminate bad ones." This intelligence shows not in flashes of insight but in framework design — and once the framework is designed, compute can execute it in bulk. So compute is not a replacement for the smart person; compute is the smart person's leverage — provided the person knows what to falsify with it. The Deputy did not understand any of this; he saw only "people," not "systems."

But he would almost certainly attribute quant results entirely to people. In his cognition, whether a strategy is good depends on "whether the person doing it is good" — nothing to do with how fast the computer is. This is consistent with primary-market logic: whether a project succeeds depends on "how strong the relationship is," not on tools. So he would spend 5000 yuan on a dinner for someone who might bring a project, but would not spend 5000 yuan to add memory to a computer. In his ledger, people are worth more than machines — because people can drink, toast, say "this project is not bad" at the dinner table. A computer only quietly runs code, then tells you — this factor has failed. That was not the answer he wanted.

He did not need a faster computer; he needed a faster performance.

The Deputy called the backtest script the PhD had written a "system." I froze for a moment when I heard it. Something without complete function encapsulation, without tests, without documentation, without version control, called a "system." But I did not correct him. On one hand, what the PhD had written did not even qualify as a good script — explaining the difference between "script" and "system" would have been casting pearls before swine. On the other hand, the misnomer itself was telling — in the Deputy's cognition, any code that could produce a curve was a "system." He did not know what a system was, but he needed a word for the "thing that produces curves" he was looking at, and "system" sounded more advanced than "script," more valuable than "script," more like something that could be reported as a business.

A backtest script being called a system, and a 5000-yuan computer being treated as quant infrastructure, are two manifestations of the same cognitive blind spot: he could not tell tool from system, just as he could not tell person from methodology.

## Gesticulating

The PhD and the Deputy shared a trait — both liked to gesticulate. This was not meaningless habit; it was cognitive gap-filling behavior. When a person cannot express an idea in precise language, the hands take over for the mouth — the gesture draws the missing logic in the air, making the listener feel "he seems to know what he is talking about," rather than "he is saying nothing."

The PhD gesticulated because his "factor analysis" was essentially a blob of intuition that could not be mathematically defined. He needed gestures to bridge the logical chasm between "this factor works" and "the curve I drew looks decent." The denser the gestures, the more invisible the chasm.

The Deputy gesticulated because he did not understand quant at all, but needed to maintain the "I also get it" performance in front of the PhD and the shareholders. He mimicked the PhD's gestures like a parrot mimicking a human voice — not because he understood what those gestures represented, but because he had observed that people who gestured looked more credible.

After the PhD left, the Deputy fell silent. Their performance was a two-person play — the PhD gestured to explain factors, the Deputy gestured to echo "right, right, right, this direction is good." When the lead left, the supporting actor had no lines of his own. He did not dare gesture anymore, because no one was drawing the vague shape in the air first for him to trace.

This was a symbiosis of cognitive parasitism — the PhD parasitized the Boss's resources, the Deputy parasitized the PhD's performance. When the parasitic chain broke, the Deputy became a man without a host.

## The Deputy's World of Subjective Trading

After the PhD left, the Deputy returned to his comfort zone — subjective-trading narratives. His survival logic was not "make the right investment" but "not embarrass himself at any dinner."

"Heard some news" — he was not analyzing information; he was collecting narratives that excited him. Whether the news was true did not matter; what mattered was whether it gave him, for a moment, the feeling "I know something others do not."

"The myth of the double" — he was not making a probability judgment; he was anchoring on an extreme. He had heard a story about a double, and turned that story into a possibility. The story excited him, so he believed it.

"Reading K-lines can make money" — he was not doing technical analysis; he was doing pattern hallucination. The human brain is wired to see patterns in random noise — dragons in clouds, head-and-shoulders in K-lines.

"Catch-up demand" — these four characters were not his original; he had heard them from someone else's mouth at a dinner. The other party was at some "macro hedge fund" doing research, and that label was his knowledge certification. He did not need to verify whether the concept held statistically; he only needed to know that the person saying it came from a place that sounded impressive.

His mode of knowledge acquisition was not reading papers, not writing code, not running backtests. It was hearing a word at a dinner table, and then that word becoming his analysis. He was willing to buy dinners, because dinners were his research platform.

These behaviors together formed a self-reinforcing hallucination system: hear news → get a feeling → confirm on K-lines → package with jargon → bet → if right, "I understand the market"; if wrong, "the news was bad" or "I got washed out by the market makers." Never his own methodology's problem.

## Systemic Ignorance

It was not that the Deputy was stingy, not that the PhD was paranoid — the cognitive level of the entire organization determined that they could not understand what you were doing.

In the Deputy's cognition, "quant" was "looking at K-lines on a computer," the difference from himself being only an extra screen. In the PhD's cognition, "backtesting" was "writing a script by hand and running it once," and he could not understand why an automated pipeline needed compute. In the Boss's cognition, "quant" was a fancy-sounding word used to package primary-market stories.

They did not hire because they understood quant, but because "quant sounds fancy; we should have one too." This fundamental cognitive misalignment meant — you were not driving change; you were doing something they could not possibly understand, in an organization that could not possibly understand you.

## The Structural Cause of Death for Primary-to-Secondary Transitions

Primary and secondary are completely different games. Primary: relationships → information asymmetry → exclusive channels → profit. Secondary: data → statistical tests → out-of-sample performance → profit.

Primary-to-secondary firms die in the same place: thinking resources and relationships can be transplanted, when in fact the rules have changed. The old moat — relationships, networks, exclusive channels — is worthless in the new arena. The market does not care that you are a shareholder; it does not care about your network; it only cares whether your model is right. They used primary-market thinking to do secondary-market work, like eating hotpot with knife and fork — the tool is wrong; it is not a posture problem.

## The Business Model of the Pseudo-Quant Fund

The core of such funds is not doing quant, but using the word "quant" to collect management fees. Management fees are the only certain income — whether the strategy makes money is a probability event, but management fees are a certainty. You do not need to do quant right; you only need to make clients believe you are doing quant. As long as clients do not redeem, the management fee accrues daily.

And the "smart rich" are the easiest to fool — because they believe themselves smart, so when the story is sexy enough, the speaker confident enough, the curve good-looking enough, they do not suspect they are being cheated. They suspect "the market is bad," "the strategy needs time," "this drawdown is normal." They will use their own intelligence to help the fraud explain why money was lost.

## The False Closed Loop Between the Boss and the PhD

The Boss's catchphrase: "We need returns."

The PhD thought: "Easy — I'll tune parameters and draw charts for you."

A perfect demand-supply match. The Boss wanted "returns"; he did not understand that returns require statistical testing; he only wanted the curve to go up. The PhD supplied "curves" — copied framework, tuned parameters, drew charts, curve went up, delivered. The Boss saw the curve — "We have returns!" — satisfied. The PhD got recognition, drew the next curve.

But this closed loop was not just two people — from the Boss to the Deputy to the PhD, the entire chain's cognition of quant strategy was the same sentence: **find a curve that goes straight up.** The Boss wanted curves, the Deputy wanted curves, the PhD drew curves. The three formed a perfect cognitive closed loop around the consensus of "curve." No one asked about the statistical tests behind the curve, no one asked about out-of-sample performance, no one asked about reproducibility — because none of the three had the knowledge base to pose those questions.

The PhD's manual parameter tuning had become instinct. His statistical knowledge was so poor that he could not realize his methodology had a problem — overfitting was not a risk to him; it was a concept he had never heard. Add to that his technical output to the Boss and the Deputy — every report reinforced the same loop: draw curve → Boss satisfied → Deputy echoes → I am doing it right → draw a prettier curve. This loop did not need external validation to self-reinforce, because the feedback came entirely from a Boss who did not understand quant and a Deputy who did not understand quant. **Only pretty curves can attract clients** — this was not one person's judgment; it was the team's consensus. And the cognitive level of the team determines the cognitive level of the clients it faces — a team that does not understand validation attracts clients that do not understand validation. Clients do not demand statistical tests, the team does not provide them; the team does not provide them, and clients assume they are not needed. Supply and demand agree on ignorance.

But the PhD's devotion to manual tuning had a more urgent cause. The "strategy" he and the Deputy had spent months building — that had been their flagship project, their most confident moment in front of the Boss. Then I falsified it.

The falsification process needed no complex theory — take his factors, run them out-of-sample, run statistical tests, and the results collapse on their own. But to the PhD, this was not just the strategy collapsing; it was months of his effort collapsing, it was his "technical authority" persona collapsing in front of the Boss and the Deputy. He urgently needed to prove himself again — to prove that the falsification was an "accident," to prove he could still find a "winning strategy," to prove he deserved the Boss's trust and the Deputy's dependence.

So he tuned parameters even harder, drew charts even more frequently, searched even more urgently for the next "good-looking curve." Manual tuning was not laziness; it was anxiety. He did not trust the automated pipeline, not only because he would lose authorship, but because the pipeline would falsify him again — one falsification was painful enough; a second might leave no one believing him. His entire survival strategy became an accelerating loop: tune → draw → deliver to Boss → gain recognition → temporarily safe → tune again. He was not doing research; he was doing crisis management.

And his attachment to the manual had a more basic cause — his understanding of "automation" stopped at "this is simple, use a for loop." While I was using visual programming to build modules and pipelines, he was still hand-typing low-quality code, and considered the manual way the right way. He did not understand what engineering meant — modularity, reusability, testability, extensibility — these concepts did not exist for him. His world was "write a script, run it once, get a chart"; when done, change a parameter and run again. To him, "what a for loop can solve does not need automation" — because he had no idea what problem automation was supposed to solve. He did not know that a real pipeline requires separation of data layer, factor layer, backtest layer, validation layer; did not know that each layer needs independent testing; did not know that version control is not optional decoration. Every line he hand-typed was disposable — run once, discard, rewrite next time, change a parameter, run again. This was not engineering; it was handicraft. And he considered the manual way correct — because in the world of handicraft, he was the most skilled artisan, and no one could challenge his authority.

More telling was his defensive strategy. He would refuse to use the automated system on grounds that "the pipeline has bugs" — and the so-called bugs were often problems he was used to in his hand-typed code but that would be exposed in an automated framework. In a hand script you can silently change a number, skip an exception, ignore an edge case; an automated pipeline lights up every corner. He was not fixing bugs; he was refusing inspection. He would also periodically sell the Deputy, and even finance, on "automation is unreliable" — converting a technical judgment into organizational consensus, making "manual is the safe way" the team's creed. An accountant-turned-Deputy was naturally inclined to believe "people are more reliable than machines"; finance had no need to understand why automation — they only needed to hear "unreliable," and the judgment was complete.

In fact, once automation ran successfully, it was doomsday for him — and for the Deputy. Because an automated pipeline needs no "technical authority" to hand-tune, needs no "head of quant" to report curves. The system runs itself, validates itself, reports itself. Once the system was trusted, the PhD's "technical authority" persona lost its basis, and the Deputy's "head of quant" seat lost its basis — a pipeline that can run itself does not need anyone to "manage" it. The two needed each other, and needed the manual — because only the manual could sustain the illusion that "people are needed."

This fear of "being replaced" had another piece of circumstantial evidence. The PhD and the Deputy interviewed some candidates together, but never let me participate. At the time I was building the research framework — a framework that, once built, would make their manual operations look redundant. Excluding me from interviews was not because I was unimportant; precisely because I was too important — a person who could independently judge a candidate's technical level was a variable they dared not introduce into the interview arena. Because I might point out a candidate's methodological flaws, or might discover that their two technical judgments were unreliable. More critically, an interview is a power-rehearsal field — whoever interviews defines "what qualifies as qualified." Letting me participate would be equivalent to admitting I had the right to define "qualified" — and once I had that right, the next unqualified person might be them.

The two's symbiotic logic in interviews was identical to their symbiotic logic in strategy: the PhD supplied "technical judgment" (does this person understand quant), the Deputy supplied "management judgment" (is this person a fit for the team), and the two endorsed each other, jointly sustaining the narrative "we have the ability to judge people." Any third party's entry would break this closed loop — especially mine, someone already building a replacement framework.

More interesting was the filtering logic of the interviews themselves. The PhD mouthed that he was trying to hire a leader-level person — but the candidates actually interviewed were consistently unimpressive. This was not a hiring failure; it was an alternative definition of hiring success: he did not want "strong people"; he wanted "people weak enough but who looked all right." He dared not hire any real expert — experts would only expose his defects. A person who genuinely understood quant, sitting across from him, would within three sentences hear that he was overfitting, hand-tuning, drawing curves with turnover and non-operating income. What he needed was not an expert but a follower — use his framework, run his factors, do not question his methodology, do not threaten his dominance. "Leader-level" was a label for the Boss's ears; the real hiring criterion was a single line: must not be stronger than me.

More ironic was his method for assessing candidates — Leetcode. A man who could not himself write a clean script used Leetcode to assess others' engineering ability. But this was precisely his survival strategy: Leetcode has standard answers, has right and wrong, has quantifiable difficulty levels — he could use a yardstick he himself could understand and adjudicate to measure candidates, keeping himself permanently in the judge's seat. Leetcode does not test factor effectiveness, does not test out-of-sample, does not test overfitting — it tests only things where he would not expose his defects. He substituted "problem-solving ability" for "research ability" as the evaluation criterion, because the former has standard answers and the latter does not; he could adjudicate right and wrong on the former, and he himself was wrong on the latter. Up to my departure, no new hire had come on board — this was not coincidence; it was the filter faithfully executing its real function: to keep anyone who might threaten the PhD's dominance out of the team.

Looking back, what did I mean to them? I falsified their flagship strategy — the "strategy" they had spent months on, dismantled clean by out-of-sample testing and statistical tests. I did what they could not — built a research framework, set up automated pipelines, wrote code they could not write. I knew what they did not — statistical testing, orthogonalization, version control, engineering. And every day I worked in silence — did not fight for budget, did not correct misnomers, output results without emotion.

To the PhD, my existence was a walking humiliation. He did not speak, but every day he saw me building pipelines he could not understand, running tests he could not run, writing code he could not write — and his "technical authority" persona rested on the Boss and the Deputy not seeing those differences. I could see them, and I was right next to him. A silent person who could produce results without his approval was the most dangerous falsification of him — no word needed; existence itself was negation.

To the Deputy, my existence was an indigestible foreign object. He could not manage me — I brought my own computer, did not fight for budget, did not need his "management." He could not exert control through his "head of quant" identity, because he could not read what I did, could not even judge "whether what you did is right." A person he could neither manage nor read was a black hole in his map of power.

And to the symbiotic pair, my silence was the sharpest part. If I had given them a hard time, fought for resources, performed in front of the Boss — they would have known what to do, because that was a power game they were familiar with. But I did nothing — only silently worked, silently produced, silently falsified — and they did not know how to handle someone who does not play the game. A person who does not participate in the performance makes all performers uneasy — because his silence could be read as anything: contempt, pity, waiting, or mere busyness. They could not be sure, and that uncertainty was more terrifying than confrontation.

It must be said that the silence was not strategic restraint — there was simply no "whistleblowing" action to take. Whistleblowing presupposes "the Boss does not know, and I go tell the Boss." But the failure of that strategy was not something I told the Boss — the PhD and the Deputy were forced to admit it themselves. I falsified that strategy, and the result was that they had to account for it in front of the Boss. They admitted it reluctantly, and were reluctant to bring it up again — this was an embarrassment they had already digested, a page already turned. I did not need to blow the whistle; the thing itself was what they themselves had disclosed. Nor could I "blow the whistle again," because repeating something they had already admitted is not whistleblowing — it is badgering.

So silence was not a suppressed option; it was that the action "whistleblowing" did not exist in this system. What needed saying they had already said; the narrative that needed to collapse had already collapsed once in the Boss's mind — the Boss chose to continue the story not because he had not been whistleblown on, but because he needed the story to continue in the first place. I did not choose not to blow the whistle; there was no such action to perform.

There was another layer: the one-way mirror effect. They could not see what I was doing — not in the physical sense of invisible, but in the cognitive sense of incomprehension. The PhD saw me building pipelines, but did not know the engineering intent behind them; the Deputy saw me writing code, but could not even judge what that code was doing. And I could see their entire performance — their overfitting, their hand-tuning, their "computer procurement" theater, the signals they passed at lunch. This was not symmetric observation. I was a one-way mirror; they performed for themselves while also performing for me, without knowing what I was seeing. More critically, they knew I had seen through them, but did not know what I had seen through — they could sense the observation, but could not locate its content. This asymmetry of observation was itself an inversion of power: being observed without knowing what the observer sees is more unsettling than being publicly criticized. Public criticism at least tells them "I saw this," giving them something to defend against; the one-way mirror says nothing, and they can only assume I saw everything — and the worst version is usually the easiest to assume.

Another layer: the potential threat is undefendable. I did not challenge them, did not blow the whistle to the Boss, did not try to replace them — as noted, whistleblowing did not exist in this system. The threat was entirely potential — and a potential threat cannot be defended against, because it has no attack move to respond to. They could not fire me (I was producing), could not promote me (would expose the gap), could not ignore me (I was physically present), could not co-opt me (co-optation equals acknowledging my value). Every conventional response would expose what they were trying to hide. The only thing they could do was isolate me from the interview room, to keep external gazes from reaching them through me. This was defense, not response — moving the mirror to a room no one sees, rather than wiping the image from the mirror.

Perhaps for this reason, they did not let me participate in interviews. Not because they feared I would point out candidates' problems — that was the surface. The deeper reason: they did not want candidates to see me. A silent person, obviously better at quant than them, sitting in the interview room, was itself a silent dismantling of their "technical authority" and "head of quant" personas. Candidates would notice — and would tell others on the way out. What they needed was not my technical judgment; it was my absence.

The Boss never really wanted quantitative trading. He wanted a quant story — just as the primary market needs founder stories. The PhD's overfit curve was not a bug; it was a feature — it was the quant version of the founder persona: title, story, performance, deliverable.

From start to finish, the Boss consumed secondary-market output using primary-market narrative logic. What he wanted was not alpha; it was an alpha story he could brag about.

## The Trust Structure of the Primary Market

Why does the primary market naturally reject validation? Because its entire trust structure is built on people, not on method.

The primary market is a highly uncertain environment. You invest in an unprofitable company, an unvalidated business model, a story that may not survive to the next round. In such an environment, math is useless — no historical data, no statistical significance. The only thing you can hold onto is the person: is this person reliable? Is his résumé good? Is he charismatic when he speaks?

So the primary market formed a trust system anchored on people:

Titles replaced validation. The PhD's title needed no validation, because "physics PhD" was itself a signal — whether he did optoelectronic materials or theoretical physics, the audience did not care and would not check.

Aura replaced evidence. A man speaking with verve at a dinner was more credible than a man silently writing code at a computer, because "confidence" is ability in the primary market — you cannot judge his model, but you can judge his presence.

Relationships replaced methodology. Who a piece of news came from mattered more than whether the news was accurate. "A researcher at a macro hedge fund said so" — the logic of this sentence is: because he is in that circle, what he says is right. This is a chain of trust, not a chain of evidence.

The primary market's lack of reverence for math was not arrogance; it was fear. If mathematical validation were introduced, the entire trust chain would collapse — you would find that every project you invested in had indeterminate endogeneity problems, that every founder you believed in had a résumé that could not withstand scrutiny. Math is not a tool; math is a demon-revealing mirror. And the primary market does not need a demon-revealing mirror — it needs a makeup artist.

This is why the PhD survived in the primary market. He was not providing quant capability — he was providing makeup services. His PhD title was the Boss's foundation, his overfit curve was the Boss's powder, his gesticulation was the Boss's lipstick.

And my presence made them uncomfortable not because I did anything wrong, but because I stood next to the makeup artist holding a demon-revealing mirror. I asked "does this factor have statistical significance"; I asked "does this strategy have out-of-sample validation" — every question I asked was asking "is this makeup fake." Tearing off the makeup in front of the makeup artist is of course unwelcome.

## This Pattern Is Not Limited to the Potemkin Village

In business schools, swarms of students running off to hear a chief economist with an astronomical salary lecture, and the Boss bringing the PhD to meet LPs, are essentially the same thing. What the students go to hear is not "is this model right" but "this person is impressive" — title, aura, presence, no different from the PhD's gesticulation. What they need is not knowledge but safety: if the big shot said so, then my paper direction should be fine, right? If the big shot judged so, then I can cite it in an interview, right?

But the problem is — the big shot himself may not have read many annual reports either. The chief economist's core skill is not forecasting; it is narrative. He needs to compress complex economic phenomena into a story tellable at a dinner; precision does not matter, transmissibility does. This is exactly the same as the PhD reducing quant to "save plot" — not that they do not want to get it right, but that their audiences do not require them to get it right.

The audience does not need validation, so the performer does not need to get it right.

But honestly, statisticians may not be cleaner than performers. Everyone essentially needs to get something from the audience — the chief economist needs adoration, the PhD needs resources and dominance, the Boss needs clients' money. The difference is only in tools: some perform, some do statistics. Statistics can produce real insights, and can also be used to manufacture more refined hallucinations — p-hacking, data mining, overfitting are all performance forms of statistics.

The only difference is — statistics are honest, at least falsifiable. You may not like a p-value, but you cannot make it disappear. You can switch windows, switch samples, switch model specifications, but every operation leaves a trace — reviewers can find it, readers can reproduce it, later researchers can overturn it. The underlying contract of statistics is not "I prove I am right," but "I tell you how I did it; you can reproduce it under the same conditions; if the result differs, I am wrong."

Performance has no such contract. The PhD's overfit curve does not need to tell you how the parameters were tuned; the chief economist's lecture does not need to tell you how many forecasts were wrong; the Boss's dinner does not need to tell you whether that "catch-up demand" ever actually caught up. Their assertions are unfalsifiable — not because they are too smart, but because they have never given conditions that can be tested.

## The Mirror Effect

I may have said nothing, but my presence itself made them uncomfortable. The Deputy and the PhD liked to hold forth on their market operations — "I called this one right," "I have experience with that factor." I sat beside, quietly writing code, building pipelines, running backtests.

My presence itself was a negation of their narrative. I did not need to say a word; my way of working was saying "what you are saying, I can test with code." When a man is bragging and someone who can verify the brag stands next to him, the brag stops.

So they needed to make me disappear — not physically, but in voice. No computer for you, no pipeline for you, opposition to your automation. Not because you did something wrong, but because you did something right. Your presence meant they could no longer lie.

## Leaving

I did not leave voluntarily. The Deputy told me: quant is done; we are dissolving.

I was not fired — the entire quant unit was cut. The PhD went to Huawei, the Deputy went back to subjective trading, the Boss went back to the primary market. They scattered not because someone left, but because this organization had never actually done quant; dissolution was just a return to its original state.

And I, the moment I got the dissolution notice, felt relief.

I did not need this job to prove myself; I already had my own work. This job was not a livelihood to me; it was the runtime environment for a diagnostic program. The diagnostic program finished running, the conclusions were output, the runtime environment closed — this was not failure; it was a normal exit.

I was not driven out, and I did not flee. Before they dissolved, I had already finished what I came to do. By the time they dissolved, I was already packing my things.

I left a wrong organization, and then used the counter-example of that organization to build an entire correct system. This was not "leaving"; it was distilling error into methodology.

## Finally

In the end, the wall between primary-market players and quant is not a methodological wall; it is a worldview wall. In that world, monologue can replace rigor, relationships can replace ability, capital can replace cognition.

I was there, I saw, I left.

Any information that cannot be independently validated is, in essence, noise. This is the most important thing I learned at the Potemkin village.
